How the Budget model works

The game is a toy, but it is not arbitrary. Every relationship in it corresponds to something the Treasury actually argues about. Here is the whole thing, with the numbers — including the parts it gets wrong on purpose, and the parts it cannot represent at all.

What you are steering

You deliver five Budgets, one a year from 2026 to 2031, and then there is a general election. Six levers: income tax and National Insurance, VAT and corporation tax on one side; day-to-day departmental spending, welfare uprating and capital investment on the other. Each is a change to a standing level, measured in billions of pounds a year.

Six numbers judge you — growth, inflation, unemployment, borrowing, debt as a share of GDP, and your poll lead — but only one of them decides whether you keep the job.

The single most important rule

The fiscal impulse is the change in the level, not the level. Raise day-to-day spending by £40bn and you get one good year. The following year that £40bn is the baseline, the boost has gone, and the bill has not. To get the same boost again you have to find another £40bn, and then another.

This is not a game mechanic invented to stop you spending. It is why "£X billion extra for the NHS" stops being growth after twelve months, and it is the reason a government can increase spending every year of a parliament and still preside over a slowing economy.

The two things you cannot see

The rate game hides the neutral rate, because nobody can observe it. This one hides two numbers, for the same reason.

The fiscal multiplier

How much output does a pound of government spending actually buy? Serious economists put it anywhere between 0.4 and 1.5 for the same pound of the same spending. That spread is not ignorance that better data would close — the answer genuinely depends on how much slack there is, on what the central bank does in response, and on how much of the money leaks straight out into imports.

So the game draws a true value at the start and never shows it to you. Hiding it is not a difficulty setting, it is the actual condition of the job. In the model it is bigger when there is slack:

multiplier = true value × (1 − 0.18 × output gap)

Idle workers and idle factories mean a pound of new demand meets a supply response. An economy already running hot meets a price response instead. This is why the same Budget is stimulus in one year and inflation in the next, and it is the finding that made the IMF revise its view of European austerity in 2013.

The OBR's productivity forecast

Everything you are judged against rests on one number: trend productivity growth. The Office for Budget Responsibility publishes an estimate, the game opens it at 1.15%, and the truth is drawn separately and never shown. Each year the estimate is marked partway towards the truth, and nothing you do moves it.

When it is marked down, your headroom against the fiscal rule does not shrink — it disappears. A downgrade of 0.2 percentage points compounds over five years into roughly £14bn of receipts that were never going to arrive. You will feel that as unfair. It is also, more or less exactly, what happened to every Chancellor of the last fifteen years.

The fiscal rule, and headroom

The hard constraint is that debt must be falling as a share of GDP by the final year of the forecast. Headroom is the amount of extra borrowing a year you could take on and still just satisfy it. The game starts you with about £10bn of it, which sounds like a lot and is not: it is roughly 0.3% of GDP, or about four days of government spending. Rachel Reeves's actual margin in March 2025 was £9.9bn.

The figure the game shows you while you drag the sliders is computed on the OBR's assumptions, not on what turns out to be true. It is a forecast. Next year it will have been wrong. That gap is the whole game.

The Bank of England, inverted

In the rate game you are the Governor and the Monetary Policy Committee only advises. Here it is a reaction function, it is automatic, and you cannot overrule it:

Bank Rate → 3.5% + 1.5 × (inflation − 2%) + 0.5 × output gap

Loosen hard enough and the Bank takes the growth back off you through the mortgage market. There is one deliberate subtlety: the Bank looks through only half of a VAT rise, which is roughly its stated practice on indirect taxes. So a Chancellor who raises VAT to fund a giveaway pays for it twice — once at the till, and once in everybody's mortgage.

The bond market

Gilt yields do not respond to how much you borrow. They respond to how much more than expected you borrow, and they respond far harder when the debt ratio is already rising, because that is the point at which the market starts wondering whether anyone intends to stop.

gilt yield += 0.012 × borrowing surprise × (1.8 if debt is rising)

The yield itself mean-reverts. The damage does not. About a tenth of the debt stock reprices each year, so a spike keeps arriving in the interest bill for a decade after the market has calmed down. The UK's saving grace here is real: it has the longest average debt maturity in the G7, at about fourteen years, which is why a gilt crisis is survivable at all.

The snowball

Debt as a share of GDP moves on the difference between the average interest rate on the stock and nominal growth:

new ratio = old ratio × (1 + (interest rate − nominal growth)/100) + borrowing/GDP

If the rate exceeds growth, the ratio rises even with the budget balanced. This is why cutting so hard that growth stops can raise the debt ratio you were cutting to lower. The denominator is half the argument, and it is the half that gets forgotten.

Scarring

Run the economy far enough below capacity for long enough and some of the damage never reverses. Skills go stale, people leave the workforce altogether, firms that would have invested do not. The jobless floor rises permanently and easing later does not bring it back. In the game this is the heaviest single penalty per year, deliberately: a scoring rule that treated permanent lost output as a rounding error would be teaching the wrong lesson.

What the voters see

Not the debt ratio. Real pay, whether anyone they know has lost a job, and how much tax they pay. Borrowing is invisible to them, and that is the trap the game is built around: the thing that destroys you in year five is the thing nobody punishes you for in year one.

Two asymmetries are deliberate. A welfare cut costs more support than an equivalent rise gains, because a cut produces named people on the news and a rise produces nobody. And corporation tax is nearly free at the point of decision — its entire cost arrives later, as business investment that did not happen and growth nobody can attribute to anything.

How par is set

Par is measured, not guessed. Six programmatic strategies were run over 1,500 seeded games each — do nothing, a funded capital-tilted Budget, borrow-and-spend, austerity, a supply-side package, and the one that taxes companies to pay for welfare. Par is then set so that the funded, competent Budget beats it about ten times in twelve.

Par moves with the hand you were dealt, because a term containing a banking crisis and a productivity downgrade cannot be judged against a calm one:

par = 16 − 34 × crises − 7 × major shocks − 55 × productivity downgrade

Those sweeps also confirm the two things the model most needed to get right: borrow-and-spend loses to the bond market, and austerity loses to scarring. Both lose for the stated reason, not because they were penalised for it.

What it deliberately leaves out

No distinction between the different taxes' incidence across the income distribution, which is most of what a real Budget argument is about. No devolved administrations, no local government, no distinction between current and capital departmental limits, no demographics and so no honest account of the thing that actually drives long-run spending. No exchange rate you can see, no housing market, no banking system, and no private sector balance sheet. The whole yield curve is one number. Public services exist only as a quantity of money, so there is no such thing in this model as spending it badly.

It also cannot represent the single most important constraint on a real Chancellor, which is that a Budget has to get through a Cabinet, a party and a Parliament. In the game the levers simply move.

Real fiscal forecasting models run to hundreds of equations and still miss by tens of billions. This one has about seven, and the point of it is the trade-off, not the forecast.
